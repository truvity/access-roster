# 0017 — Each boundary has its own issuer, a token has one audience, people sign in with passkeys and step-up, and break-glass recovery uses passkeys held in config

**Status:** Accepted; amended by 0045
**Date:** 2026-11-12

Proposal: [issue #909](https://github.com/truvity/sluis/issues/909).

## Context

v1 was one installation with one token issuer and one signing key set. Every relying party, in every environment, trusted the same key, so a compromised signer or a policy bug could mint a token for production as easily as for development.

v2 is a federation of boundaries ([0008](/decisions/0008-boundaries-and-tenants/)). One front installation gives people one sign-in URL, one console and one identity plane. Each environment boundary has its own keys and its own issuer, and is joined to the front by declared trust. A company is a tenant of an environment boundary ([0008](/decisions/0008-boundaries-and-tenants/#tenants)): it owns directories, upstreams and people records, but holds no authority key. sluis hosts no end-user accounts and stores no passwords: people come from upstream directories ([REQ-CIAM-017](/requirements/ciam/#REQ-CIAM-017)).

Constraints from outside:

- OpenID Connect requires `iss` to be an https URL, with discovery under it at `/.well-known/openid-configuration`, and not every relying party accepts an issuer with a path.
- A passkey belongs to a relying-party ID, a domain that the page's web origin must equal or sit under, short of a public suffix.
- AWS IAM OIDC providers check `aud` against a list of client IDs; the default `sts.amazonaws.com` is the same for every account.
- Upstream multi-factor authentication on every sign-in is slow, and sluis cannot ask the upstream for a fresh factor on its own terms. WebAuthn, RFC 9470 (step-up) and RFC 8628 (device login) are the published answers.

Operators sign in through an upstream directory. When the directory is down, a federation is misconfigured or no upstream can vouch for anyone, they still need a way back in to repair it. That way in is the one exception to "no accounts", so it has to be narrow, visible, proven to work, and independent of the data that may be the thing that is damaged. v1 offered two recovery proofs: a Kubernetes ServiceAccount token checked by the API server, and an SRP-6a password that never crossed the wire ([#795](https://github.com/truvity/sluis/issues/795)). Both are reusable secrets, or depend on a system that may be the one that failed. WebAuthn gives a phishing-resistant proof whose private half never leaves its authenticator, and an attestation statement can show that an authenticator is hardware. The tooling that enrols such credentials was measured in a spike ([#1178](https://github.com/truvity/sluis/issues/1178)) on 2026-11-03: a hardware FIDO key, a PIV slot and a synced passkey were each enrolled and verified, and its findings are the decisions below.

Staff passkeys had no class: a passkey synced through a platform account and a key that never leaves a security key counted the same. NIST SP 800-63B-4 (final, 2025-07-31) allows exportable keys at authenticator assurance level 2 (AAL2) and forbids syncable authenticators at AAL3 ([AAL requirements](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b/aal/), read 2026-11-06). A synced passkey gives no attestation, and an attestation is the only way a verifier learns what an authenticator is (the Microsoft Entra passkey documentation, updated 2026-06-15, says so for the Apple and Google passkey providers). A workforce also has a few people whose sign-in can move keys, identities and releases, and for them the account that syncs a passkey is a path to the credential. The maintainer decided on 2026-11-06 to run two tiers, to adopt the class model below, to switch enforcement on later, and to settle three details: an upstream claim never satisfies a signing or key-administration grant, the AAGUID allowlist lives in `SignInMethods` and a tenant can only narrow it, and the class `hardware` gets neither `hwk` nor `swk` ([Two tiers of staff sign-in](/decisions/0017-identity-and-sign-in/#two-tiers-of-staff-sign-in); the feature is [#885](https://github.com/truvity/sluis/issues/885)).

Tokens are standard JWS and JWT, signed by the token-signing keys of [0006](/decisions/0006-keys-and-sealing/#the-token-signing-key). The actors are those of [0002](/decisions/0002-families-of-actors/#the-four-families).

## Decision

### Issuers

- **Each environment boundary has its own issuer, and the front has a group issuer.** An internal installation with kernel, devel, stage and prod boundaries has five issuers. A customer boundary holds keys and serves the issuers of its tenants.
- **Every tenant has an issuer of its own.** Its host is the login domain of the tenant ([0035](/decisions/0035-customer-pages/#login-domains)), under the operator's domain or the tenant's own, with discovery and the key set served there from static documents. A boundary serves one or more such issuers. A tenant's issuer signs with the token-signing key of its location: its boundary's key, or a key of its own in the customer's account for a tenant that keeps its keys there ([0006](/decisions/0006-keys-and-sealing/#where-a-tenants-keys-live)). Client IDs stay unique per issuer. A relying party pins the issuer of its client's tenant, and a tenant's move keeps it ([0032](/decisions/0032-customer-identity-installations/#moving)).
- **An issuer is a name and a key set, not a boundary.** A tenant's `iss` reveals no organisation beyond the host that the tenant chose.
- **Each issuer has its own host, one host is one web origin, and the issuer URL has no path.** The group issuer is `https://sluis.example.com`; an environment boundary's issuer is `https://sluis.<env>.example.com`. A test installation lives outside these names. Discovery is served at `/.well-known/openid-configuration` and at `/.well-known/oauth-authorization-server`, one document at both (OpenID Connect Discovery 1.0 section 4, RFC 8414 section 3); an issuer that has a path, which only a test installation has, is also served at both names with its path inserted after them (RFC 8414 section 3.1), and at no other path.
- **Discovery and the key set are static documents,** one set per issuer, in a bucket of that boundary, so a relying party verifies tokens while the issuer's compute is down. The key set is the exportable kind `SigningRing`, which `signer` writes and exporter@ publishes as public material ([0009](/decisions/0009-export/#what-is-exportable)). It lists every token key that is pre-published, active or retiring, each with its `x5c` chain ([0006](/decisions/0006-keys-and-sealing/#rotation)). An edge cache in front of it, its fallback to the live key set of `entry` and its purge are in [0009](/decisions/0009-export/#the-edge). The discovery document has no kind: IaC renders it from the boundary's config and publishes it beside the key set, because exporter@ exports only the kinds of [0009](/decisions/0009-export/#what-is-exportable).
- **Lifetimes are set per issuer** by the config kind `TokenLifetime`, under the session classes of [0018](/decisions/0018-sessions/); the token lifetimes and their caps are in [0020](/decisions/0020-grant-vocabulary-and-token-claims/#lifetimes).

### Audiences

- **`iss` is the issuer URL. `aud` is exactly one registered `client_id`, never a URL.**
- **A resource indicator (RFC 8707 `resource=`) selects a registered client.** It is never copied into `aud`.
- **Between actors of different boundaries, `aud` is the target's SPIFFE ID**, and the call runs over mutual TLS ([0022](/decisions/0022-workload-identity-and-host-enrolment/#spiffe-identities)).
- **Client IDs are unique per issuer and readable, with the same name in every environment**, such as `kubernetes` or `argocd`. A client ID names the relying party that accepts the token. How clients are declared is [0021](/decisions/0021-clients/#the-client-declaration).
- **An AWS role is a relying party, so each role is a client:** `aws:<account>:<role>`, where `<account>` is the account's name as the client's declaration gives it (for example `aws:example-prod:deployer`), never the account number. The role's trust policy pins that `aud`, so which person or workload may assume which role is decided by sluis's policy and carried in the token, and AWS holds no per-person statement. Each account's IAM OIDC provider lists the client IDs of its roles.
- **A token exchange into a boundary uses that boundary's client ID at the group issuer.**
- **Every relying party pins both `iss` and `aud`.** sluis prints reference configuration for each kind of relying party, and the infrastructure code applies it.

### Subjects

- **A person has an opaque random ID, and the `sub` of a token is derived from it by the tenant's sector** ([0034](/decisions/0034-end-users-and-upstream-signals/#subjects-and-sectors)). `session` assigns the ID when it first sees a person: at a directory read where the upstream has a directory, so it is known before the first sign-in, and otherwise at the person's first sign-in. The first working version signs people in through the generic OpenID Connect upstream, sign-in only, so its subjects are assigned at first sign-in ([#1106](https://github.com/truvity/sluis/issues/1106)); directory sync, and with it the subject before the first sign-in, comes in the parity milestone ([#805](https://github.com/truvity/sluis/issues/805)). It is never an e-mail address or a directory account name. An upstream account is tied to a person by the issuer and a configured subject claim, never by e-mail ([0019](/decisions/0019-person-and-upstream-link/)).
- **The directory account and `email` are claims of their own.** Tokens carry `acr`, `amr` and `auth_time`, and `cnf.jkt` when the audience declares binding to a DPoP key; an audience that declares none gets a bearer token ([0020](/decisions/0020-grant-vocabulary-and-token-claims/#binding)).
- **A token carries only the grant names of its own boundary.** Grant names are declared in a vocabulary and narrowed per audience; the vocabulary, the claims of a token and its lifetimes are [0020](/decisions/0020-grant-vocabulary-and-token-claims/).
- **Workload tokens come from the same issuer.** A JWT-SVID has the boundary's URL as `iss` and the workload's `spiffe://` ID as `sub` ([0022](/decisions/0022-workload-identity-and-host-enrolment/#spiffe-identities)).
- **Which `sub` a client sees is its tenant's sector,** declared in the `Tenant` item and inherited by the client. One controller's apps see the same `sub`, and a regulated audience never sees the public one. The rule is [0034](/decisions/0034-end-users-and-upstream-signals/#subjects-and-sectors).

### Passkeys

- **Passkeys are the fast sign-in path and count as phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication.** They are credentials of staff who come from a directory; sluis stores only their public keys, as the durable kind `StaffPasskey` that `session` writes, sealed in the person's tenant, with the class of [The passkey class](/decisions/0017-identity-and-sign-in/#the-passkey-class).
- **A full upstream sign-in, with the upstream's own multi-factor authentication, is still required** every N days. Policy sets N and the rules. A high-privilege grant name needs the privileged tier of [Two tiers of staff sign-in](/decisions/0017-identity-and-sign-in/#two-tiers-of-staff-sign-in), not the upstream's multi-factor authentication alone.
- **Staff passkeys and the passkey flows use one relying-party ID,** the shared sign-in domain declared in the config kind `FrontHost`. It is the registrable parent domain, `example.com`, because the boundary hosts are siblings of the group host, so one passkey works on every sluis host.
- **A passkey is added by a fresh sign-in at the upstream, held to the rule of one client the operator names (decided 2026-11-08).** `GET /passkeys/add` takes no parameter and sends the person to the upstream again, with `prompt=login` and `max_age=0`. The callback admits them only for an authentication made because of that request and only if policy allows `token.issue` for the client that `SignInMethods` names in `registration_client`; the same decision lets `session` write the passkey, and it is made again when the browser's answer arrives. The field is required whenever passkeys are on, and a set that names a client the installation does not declare, or one that does not sign people in, is refused at load. The pinned client's `requires` grants decide who may register, so a dedicated registration client, whose rule is the one the operator wants for adding a factor, is the cleaner pin than a client borrowed from an application. The operator, not the request, chooses the rule a factor is added under; a request cannot ask which clients exist. The local installation names `example-app`, the client with its strictest rule (multi-factor authentication no older than 15 minutes). A dedicated policy action for a registration, `passkey.enrol`, may come later, so that the rule is not borrowed from a client; until then a registration is held to the rule of a client.
- **End-user passkeys for customers belong to the upstream.** A client's own login domain and its branding are [0035](/decisions/0035-customer-pages/)'s.
- **Every verifier keeps a strict allow-list of web origins:** the group host, the boundary hosts and their audit hosts ([0028](/decisions/0028-audit-pieces/#the-audit-front)), nothing else. An assertion from any other origin under the parent domain is refused.
- **Passkeys are tested from outside the product:** a browser's virtual authenticator and a software authenticator library drive the released binary. There is no test code in sluis.
- **Break-glass recovery has its own credentials,** held in config ([Break-glass recovery](/decisions/0017-identity-and-sign-in/#break-glass-recovery)). Session classes and limits are [0018](/decisions/0018-sessions/).

### Two tiers of staff sign-in

- **Enforced: later.** The tiers and the rules below are accepted. From the first version `session` records the class of every staff passkey, stores the raw attestation object of every registration, and puts `acr` and `amr` into tokens. The attestation verifier arrives at the parity milestone and re-verifies the stored objects, so a key enrolled before then is classified when the verifier lands and nobody registers again when enforcement starts. Until then such a key is `hardware` or `synced` by its BE flag, and is never `hardware-attested` ([REQ-AUTHN-080](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-080), [REQ-AUTHN-096](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-096)). The policy rules that demand the privileged tier are written as reviewed policy and switched on by committing them, so turning enforcement on is a policy change and no code change. Until the maintainer commits them, no grant demands the privileged tier and every staff passkey of every class signs in as before.
- **The ordinary tier: everyone signs in with a phishing-resistant method, and a synced passkey is enough.** This is AAL2 of NIST SP 800-63B-4. A staff passkey of any class meets it, and sluis asks for no attestation of it ([REQ-AUTHN-083](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-083)).
- **The privileged tier: a person who administers identity, keys, organisation settings or releases signs in with a device-bound, attested security key.** The privileged grants are signing, key administration, identity administration, organisation settings and releases. The installation's policy names the grant names and clients that demand the tier. The tier is a property of the sign-in: a person who holds such a grant meets it with a key of class `hardware-attested`, and signs in to everything else as before.
- **The tier is modelled on AAL3 and does not claim it.** A non-exportable key that resists phishing is the part of AAL3 that a verifier can check; sluis does not require a FIPS-validated authenticator and claims no conformance.
- **Break-glass recovery is exempt.** It keeps its own credentials and its own rules ([Break-glass recovery](/decisions/0017-identity-and-sign-in/#break-glass-recovery)), among them the pinned synced passkey of [REQ-AUTHN-069](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-069). The tier rules never apply to a recovery sign-in, and a recovery session carries the recovery scope only.

### The passkey class

Every `StaffPasskey` has a class, set at registration and stored with the credential's AAGUID, the raw attestation object and the outcome of its attestation check ([REQ-AUTHN-080](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-080)).

| Class | How it is derived | `amr` that sluis adds |
|---|---|---|
| `hardware-attested` | The registration carried an attestation statement of a kind that proves a vendor key, verified to a pinned vendor attestation root that is bound to the AAGUID, the AAGUID is on the installation's allowlist, and the backup eligible flag (BE) is clear ([REQ-AUTHN-081](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-081)) | `mfa`, `hwk` |
| `hardware` | No verified attestation (none was sent, it did not verify, or the AAGUID is not on the allowlist), and BE is clear. The key may be device-bound, but only the authenticator says so | `mfa` |
| `synced` | BE is set, whatever the attestation says | `mfa`, `swk` |

- **The class comes from three things:** the attestation statement and the AAGUID allowlist, which decide whether a device-bound key was proven, and the flags BE and BS (backup state) in the authenticator data. BE says the credential may be backed up and is fixed for the life of a credential; BS says whether it is backed up now. BE wins: a credential with BE set is `synced` even when an attestation statement is present, because a backed-up key has left its device.
- **Each assertion is checked against the class.** An assertion whose BE differs from the registered one is refused, and so is one with BS set and BE clear, which WebAuthn defines as invalid ([REQ-AUTHN-089](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-089)). Both flags are checked at registration too: BE clear with BS set is refused there as well ([REQ-AUTHN-089](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-089)). A class changes in place only by re-verifying the stored attestation object ([REQ-AUTHN-096](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-096), [Revoked vendor keys](/decisions/0017-identity-and-sign-in/#the-passkey-class)); a person who wants a better class otherwise registers a new credential.
- **`amr` never claims more than sluis verified.** `hwk` only for `hardware-attested` ([REQ-AUTHN-085](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-085)), `swk` only for `synced` ([REQ-AUTHN-088](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-088)), and neither for `hardware`. The `acr` is the installation's own value for the tier that the assertion met.
- **The AAGUID allowlist is installation config** in the config kind `SignInMethods`: the models the installation issues to its privileged people, each certified in the FIDO Metadata Service (MDS3), as a `hardware-fido` credential must be. A tenant may only narrow it ([REQ-AUTHN-082](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-082)). It is a control only because attestation is verified; an AAGUID alone is self-asserted.
- **Attestation is verified in five steps, all required for `hardware-attested`.**
  - **The root is bound to the AAGUID.** The pinned vendor root counts only when the FIDO MDS3 entry of that AAGUID lists it as one of its attestation roots, so a chain that is valid to some pinned root, but not the root of this model, is refused ([REQ-AUTHN-081](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-081)).
  - **The certificate names the same model.** The `id-fido-gen-ce-aaguid` extension (OID 1.3.6.1.4.1.45724.1.1.4) of the leaf certificate must be present and equal the AAGUID in the authenticator data. WebAuthn asks for the match only when the extension is present; sluis also requires it, because an allowlisted model carries it ([REQ-AUTHN-097](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-097)).
  - **Only a vendor key proves a model.** Self attestation, the `none` format and a statement with no chain never yield `hardware-attested`; the credential is `hardware` ([REQ-AUTHN-098](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-098)).
  - **The flags are consistent.** BE clear with BS set is refused at registration, as at every assertion.
  - **The model's status is current.** The MDS3 status of the AAGUID must not be a revoked or compromised one, as the next bullet says.
- **A revoked or compromised vendor attestation key is handled by re-verification.** When the MDS3 metadata marks a model's attestation key revoked or compromised, or a root leaves the pinned bundle, or the operator removes the AAGUID from the allowlist, sluis refuses new `hardware-attested` registrations of that model and re-verifies the stored attestation objects at the next metadata refresh. An affected credential falls to `hardware`, so every grant that demands the tier asks its holder for a key of another model ([REQ-AUTHN-099](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-099), [REQ-AUTHN-100](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-100)). How often the metadata is refreshed is config.
- **The attestation verifier is the one of break-glass,** with the same pinned vendor roots ([REQ-AUTHN-068](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-068), [0024](/decisions/0024-root-custody-and-the-signing-api/)).
- **No enterprise attestation.** sluis asks for conveyance `direct` at most and never for `enterprise` ([REQ-AUTHN-084](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-084)). It keeps a model, never a per-device identifier such as a serial number, which would let sites correlate a person's key. An organisation that wants an inventory of keys keeps a purchase register by hand.
- **The signature counter is checked only for credentials that are not `synced`** ([REQ-AUTHN-052](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-052)). A credential with BE set legitimately reports counter 0 or a counter that does not rise, so a regression is no sign of a clone, and a check would refuse real sign-ins. `hardware-attested` and `hardware` keep the check, as `hardware-fido` does for break-glass. A device-bound authenticator that always returns counter 0 is accepted when the stored counter is also 0, because WebAuthn defines two zeros as "no counter" and not as a regression; a counter that was above 0 and falls is refused.

### Enrolling a key

- **Once a person holds a `hardware-attested` key, adding any further credential needs a fresh `hardware-attested` assertion by that person** ([REQ-AUTHN-091](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-091)). Attestation proves that a key is device-bound, not whose device it is: without this rule, anyone holding a person's synced-passkey session could register their own allowlisted key and reach the privileged tier. The rule covers a credential of every class, and registering the person's second hardware key. "Fresh" is the `max_age` of the policy, as for a privileged grant.
- **The first `hardware-attested` key of a person needs approval by an operator.** The approver holds the identity-administration grant, is a different person from the enrollee, and approves with a fresh `hardware-attested` assertion of their own ([REQ-AUTHN-092](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-092)). A directory approval is not accepted: the directory cannot say whose device a key is. The first operator of an installation has no approver and is named in the config kind `FirstAdmin`, a reviewed commit.
- **Every such enrolment is audited, and raises an alert to the operators** ([REQ-AUTHN-093](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-093), [REQ-AUTHN-094](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-094)), whether it succeeds or is refused. An unexpected hardware key is an incident until proven otherwise.
- **Break-glass credentials are outside this rule,** because they are config and change only by a signed commit ([Break-glass recovery](/decisions/0017-identity-and-sign-in/#break-glass-recovery)).

### Fresh privileged sign-in

- **A privileged grant needs a fresh sign-in at the `hardware-attested` tier, through RFC 9470 step-up** ([REQ-AUTHN-086](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-086)). The rule is a `forbid` with `@step_up("acr_values=<hardware tier> max_age=<seconds>")` ([Policy](/reference/policy/#step-up)); `session` answers `insufficient_user_authentication` with those values, and the client signs in again with a key of that class.
- **"Fresh" is a sign-in age.** It is `auth_age`, the seconds since the person's latest authentication, which for this rule must itself be a `hardware-attested` assertion. A sign-in upstream, or with a synced passkey, resets nothing. Policy sets the value in `max_age`, and the recommended default is 900 seconds (15 minutes), the value of the example in the policy reference.
- **The boundary verifies the assertion itself** ([Step-up](/decisions/0017-identity-and-sign-in/#step-up)). The step-up flow offers only the person's `hardware-attested` credentials. A person with none gets `unmet_authentication_requirements` ([REQ-AUTHN-009](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-009)), never a lower tier, and the token of a met step-up carries the new `acr`, `amr` and `auth_time` ([REQ-AUTHN-053](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-053)).
- **Signing and key administration are met only by an assertion that their own boundary verified itself,** the boundary that signs or that holds the key. An upstream claim never counts for these two grants, whatever the mapping table says ([REQ-AUTHN-095](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-095), [REQ-AUTHN-087](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-087)).

### What an upstream adds to a tier

- **An upstream sign-in counts toward the privileged tier only through a mapped `amr` or `acr`,** and never above the ceiling of its connection ([REQ-AUTHN-087](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-087), [REQ-AUTHN-010](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-010), [REQ-FED-028](/requirements/fed/#REQ-FED-028)). The connection's mapping table names the upstream values that stand for the tier, and its `assurance` is the cap ([0031](/decisions/0031-ciam-is-a-broker/), [#1329](https://github.com/truvity/sluis/issues/1329)). A bare `hwk` from an upstream is a hint: the upstream decides it, and sluis cannot check how the person authenticated.
- **Missing evidence is unknown, and unknown is refused.** An upstream that sends no `amr` or `acr`, or a value the mapping does not name, leaves the name out of the policy context, and the rule's `forbid` holds ([Policy](/reference/policy/#context), [REQ-TOKEN-035](/requirements/token/#REQ-TOKEN-035)). A Google sign-in carries no `amr` until [#1154](https://github.com/truvity/sluis/issues/1154) is built, so until then it never meets the privileged tier by itself.
- **A boundary's own demand is met by its own check.** An upstream claim can satisfy the tier for identity administration, organisation settings and releases where policy is evaluated from the person's session at the front. It never satisfies signing or key administration; a boundary that demands fresh authentication verifies the assertion itself, as above.

### Step-up

- **`policy@<boundary>` decides which clients need multi-factor authentication** in each environment, and how fresh it must be ([0007](/decisions/0007-policy-and-sealed-grants/)).
- **Step-up follows RFC 9470.** A relying party or the boundary answers `insufficient_user_authentication` with the `acr_values` (and `max_age`) it needs, and the client signs in again with what was asked.
- **A customer's step-up is a re-authentication at the upstream,** with the same `acr_values` and `max_age`; sluis asks no customer for a passkey ([0018](/decisions/0018-sessions/#customers)).
- **The upstream's `acr` and `amr` pass through** the generic OIDC upstream, `adapter-upstream-oidc`, and `session@<boundary>` keeps the freshness of each person's authentication for its boundary on the person's session chain ([0018](/decisions/0018-sessions/#session-chains)).
- **Each boundary runs its own slim `entry@<boundary>` with no sign-in of its own**; its upstream is the group issuer. In a browser, `entry@<boundary>` is an OpenID Connect relying party of the group issuer: it signs the person in there with the authorization code flow and PKCE, as the confidential client named after the boundary, at one exact redirect (`https://<boundary host>/callback`), and the group issuer accepts no other redirect for that client ([REQ-SESS-043](/requirements/sess/#REQ-SESS-043)). The command-line path keeps the silent token exchange below. A boundary that demands fresh multi-factor authentication **verifies the passkey assertion itself**, against its own challenge, so a compromised front cannot act there.
- **In a browser, sign-in is transparent.** A person taps a passkey only where a boundary's policy demands it.
- **On the command line there is one `sluis login`**, at the group issuer. For each environment the CLI then exchanges that token silently (RFC 8693) at the boundary's own token endpoint. The front never sees a boundary's tokens.

### Directory outages

- **When the upstream directory cannot be read, sluis serves the cached group membership up to a bounded staleness window, then fails closed.** The cache is the directory facts, temp kinds with a time to live of 24 hours by default and 7 days at most ([0019](/decisions/0019-person-and-upstream-link/#directory-facts-parity)). The window is set per installation under a maximum built into the release; past it, refresh and exchange are refused, never answered on a weaker basis.
- **A person disabled in the directory is refused at once.** Refresh and exchange stop for that person as soon as sluis learns it, whatever the window.
- Tokens already issued stay valid until they expire.

### Optional OpenID flows

sluis claims a conformance plan only when it passes, and discovery says only what is served ([Conformance](/standards/conformance/)).

- **Form post is supported** for the authorization code flow. `response_modes_supported` lists `query` and `form_post`; any other `response_mode` is refused with `invalid_request`. The auto-submit page carries a strict Content-Security-Policy that allows its one script by the script's hash or by a fresh nonce (decided 2026-11-04: for a fixed script a hash protects as well as a nonce, and needs no per-response state), `form-action` limited to the redirect URI's web origin ([REQ-AUTHN-079](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-079)), no framing and no caching. The Form Post OP plan joins the conformance programme. What a client must do for it (checked in headless Chrome, 2026-11-07, for the first two points): the page posts from an opaque origin with `Referrer-Policy: no-referrer`, so the post carries `Origin: null` and no `Referer`, and a client must not rely on either to recognise it (its `state` check does the work); the client answers the post on the `redirect_uri`'s own origin, or renders its page directly, because Chromium applies `form-action` to a redirect that follows the post and blocks a redirect to another origin; and a client whose host is not ASCII registers the host in its `xn--` form, because `form-action` cannot name any other, and store@ refuses a redirect with such a host when the client is registered ([REQ-AUTHN-004](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-004)).
- **Third-party-initiated login is not served.** A client's `initiate_login_uri` is not accepted. It comes back only if the console gets an app launcher.
- **Front-channel logout is not supported.** Back-channel logout, with RP-Initiated Logout, is the logout path; discovery does not advertise front-channel logout, and a registration that names a `frontchannel_logout_uri` is refused by the configuration check.
- **CIBA is Later, in poll mode only, and only when a real consumer exists, after the parity milestone.** The person would approve with a passkey on the approving device; a notification tap alone is not an approval. Until then discovery does not advertise CIBA and its endpoint is not served.
- **Device login (RFC 8628) is postponed** ([#855](https://github.com/truvity/sluis/issues/855)); the standards register lists it as planned.
- **FAPI 2.0 comes after the parity milestone,** in the customer-identity milestone ([0038](/decisions/0038-fapi-2-profile/)).

### Break-glass recovery

- **Recovery accepts a passkey assertion and nothing else** ([REQ-AUTHN-066](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-066)): no password, no recovery code, no ServiceAccount token. sluis stores only the credential ids and the public keys.
- **The recovery credentials are config,** the kind `BreakGlassPasskey`, which `session` reads from the snapshot. A restored or damaged store cannot lose, roll back or hold back a recovery credential, because none is durable data. A credential changes only by a reviewed, signed commit ([0005](/decisions/0005-the-store/#configuration-loading)).
- **A `BreakGlassPasskey` has one of three credential classes:** `hardware-fido`, `hardware-piv` or `synced`. Enabling recovery requires three registered credentials ([REQ-AUTHN-067](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-067)):
  - **two hardware keys,** each of class `hardware-fido` or `hardware-piv`, each accepted only if its enrolment carried an attestation that was verified against the authenticator vendor's attestation roots ([REQ-AUTHN-068](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-068)), so a software copy cannot be registered and the loss of one key is survived;
  - **one synced passkey,** class `synced`, accepted only by its pinned credential id ([REQ-AUTHN-069](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-069)) and kept in a vault of its own, apart from the hardware keys, so the loss of both hardware keys is survived and no other passkey of the same account gets in. Its attestation is `none`, so the pin is the only proof, and the flags backup eligible and backup state are set.
- **A `hardware-fido` credential** is a WebAuthn credential whose packed attestation is verified to the pinned vendor root and whose model is certified in the FIDO Metadata Service (MDS3). Its assertions must show user presence and user verification, and its backup eligible and backup state flags must be clear.
- **A `hardware-piv` credential proves itself with a signature over `authenticatorData || SHA-256(clientDataJSON)`, in assertion shape,** because a PIV slot cannot speak WebAuthn. The key is generated on the device, and its attestation chain is verified to the same pinned root. User presence and user verification follow from the slot's attested policy, which must be PIN once or always and touch always; a slot with any weaker policy is refused at enrolment.
- **An enrolment is compared by its public key and its attestation chain, never by the bytes of its attestation certificate.** A PIV attestation certificate is re-issued on every request, so its bytes differ each time for the same key.
- **The enrolment tools only show the JSON to commit.** A command-line tool enrols a hardware key, `hardware-fido` or `hardware-piv`, and reads the public key of a PIV slot from its attestation. A page enrols the synced passkey: it runs on the shared sign-in domain over https, sends nothing (the content security policy sets `connect-src 'none'`) and stores nothing. The operator copies the JSON it shows into config and commits it.
- **The verify flow is the same checks for every class:** the credential id or the public key, the client data type, challenge and origin, the relying-party ID hash, the flags, and the signature. The signature counter is checked for `hardware-fido`, ignored for `hardware-piv`, and not used as a clone signal for `synced`, whose counter is 0. The same rule governs staff passkeys ([The passkey class](/decisions/0017-identity-and-sign-in/#the-passkey-class)). <!-- terms-allow: origin (the WebAuthn client-data origin) -->
- **A recovery session carries the recovery scope only** ([REQ-AUTHN-070](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-070)), and gets no refresh token. The scope is the built-in break-glass policy, which grants beyond config, so linking it is a loosening: `session` has `exchange`, the owner of `PredefinedPolicyLinkTemporary`, write the link, and it expires, after 1 hour by default and 24 hours at most ([0007](/decisions/0007-policy-and-sealed-grants/#template-links)). It restores access: an operator repairs a connection, a federation or a tenant setting. Config, the recovery credentials among it, changes only by a commit, so recovery cannot change it ([principle 17](/principles/#17-where-a-thing-is-written-decides-its-class)).
- **Every use is audited, successful or refused** ([REQ-AUTHN-071](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-071)). Recovery never waits for the audit path: the record is spooled locally and delivered when the path is back ([0029](/decisions/0029-the-audit-log/#retention)).
- **Every successful use raises an alert to the operators** ([REQ-AUTHN-072](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-072)). An unexpected recovery is an incident until proven otherwise.
- **The runbook requires a drill every quarter** ([REQ-AUTHN-073](/requirements/authn/#REQ-AUTHN-073)): each credential is used once in a test of the full path, and the drill is itself audited and alerted.
- **Recovery runs on every runtime** and needs no cloud account and no cluster: `session` verifies the assertion against its own challenge, like any passkey. `sluis config check` warns when recovery is enabled without all three credentials.
- **Recovery is exempt from the tiers.** It needs no `hardware-attested` sign-in, and its pinned synced passkey stays accepted whatever the privileged tier demands ([Two tiers of staff sign-in](/decisions/0017-identity-and-sign-in/#two-tiers-of-staff-sign-in)).
- **Recovery is for sign-in to sluis only.** Emergency SSH access to hosts is the break-glass SSH CA that ritueel holds offline ([0023](/decisions/0023-certificate-authorities/#ssh)).

### Amendment of 2026-11-12: sluis signs in staff and operators only

Maintainer decision D1 B, 2026-11-12 ([0045](/decisions/0045-customers-sign-in-upstream-and-the-sluis-token-carries-customer-org-and-product/)). sluis keeps its own sign-in, the passkeys, the hardware tiers and break-glass recovery, for staff and operators only. Customers sign in only through the upstream identity provider. The sentence under Passkeys that a client's own login domain and branding are 0035's no longer applies: 0035 is superseded, and sluis serves no customer page.

## Consequences

- A signer compromise stays in its environment: each relying party pins its own environment's issuer, so a development key cannot mint a production token.
- Five issuers mean five key sets and five sets of static documents to publish and rotate, and a DNS name and TLS certificate per host.
- A token is useful to exactly one relying party, and a token for one AWS role is refused by every other role, in that account or another. The cost is a client registration per relying party per environment.
- The CLI keeps a token per issuer under one sign-in. An outage of the front stops new sign-ins; tokens already issued keep verifying against the static key set.
- `sub` links one person across issuers without exposing an e-mail address, and does not change when the person is renamed.
- The broad passkey relying-party ID means any page under the parent domain could ask a browser for an assertion; the allow-list of web origins is therefore a security control and is tested as one. Moving sluis to another parent domain means new passkeys.
- Most sign-ins become one passkey tap that still counts as multi-factor authentication, and production can demand more than development without a code change.
- A directory outage longer than the staleness window stops sign-ins and exchanges for everyone in that directory. That is the cost of failing closed.
- Relying parties without a back-channel logout endpoint keep their sessions until they expire after a sign-out at sluis. A relying party using form post receives a cross-site POST, so it keeps its `state` check without relying on a `SameSite=Lax` cookie.
- No reusable secret exists for recovery: nothing to rotate, leak or phish, and nothing that depends on the system being recovered.
- Recovery needs three credentials committed before it is needed, and an operator who skipped that has no way back in but a restore. Adding or replacing a credential is a commit, which takes a review and a deploy; that is the price of keeping the credentials out of the data that a restore rewinds.
- Whoever holds a registered hardware key and can unlock it, or controls the synced passkey's vault, can open a recovery session. The recovery scope and its expiry bound what it can do, and the alert and the audit record make every use visible.
- The quarterly drill costs an operator's time four times a year; a recovery path that is never exercised fails when it is needed.
- Few people hold a privileged grant, so few need a security key that attests; registering one costs those people a key, not the company.
- Enforcement can start grant by grant, by a policy commit, because every passkey already carries a class. A privileged person who registered only a synced passkey is refused the grant and asked to register a key, which is the intended outcome and a visible one.
- An upstream that sends no evidence keeps a person at the ordinary tier for the front's grants, so a Google sign-in meets the privileged tier only after #1154 and an operator's mapping. The signing and key-administration grants never rely on an upstream claim.
- A synced passkey is still a credential that a platform account can recover, so the ordinary tier is only as strong as that account. This is the AAL2 trade, and the privileged tier keeps it away from the grants that matter most.
- A person who loses every hardware key cannot add a credential with a fresh hardware assertion, so recovery of a lost key is an operator approval as for a first key. A second key registered early avoids it.
- Storing the raw attestation object makes the class re-derivable, at the cost of some hundreds of bytes per credential in the sealed `StaffPasskey`.
- Verifying attestation for staff adds work to registration and a list of AAGUIDs to keep as the vendor's models change.
- The standards register and the [conformance page](/standards/conformance/) follow this record. The page [Break-glass recovery](/security/break-glass/) describes the recovery section.
- Threats addressed: T-2, T-3, T-5, T-6, T-7 and T-21, see the [threat model](/security/threat-model/).

## Roads not taken

- **One issuer for every boundary.** What v1 does; one key accepted everywhere undoes the separation of environments.
- **An issuer per environment as a path under one host.** One certificate, but every relying party must accept a path, and the issuers would share one web origin's cookies and policy.
- **Boundary hosts under the group host (`prod.sluis.example.com`).** It would narrow the passkey relying-party ID, but breaks the per-environment naming the rest of each environment uses.
- **An issuer per tenant.** Applications shared by every company would break, and it does not stop a development key from minting production tokens.
- **`aud` as a URL, several audiences, or `sts.amazonaws.com` for AWS.** Each lets a token for one party be replayed to another.
- **One AWS client per account (`aws-<account-alias>`).** A token for the account would then be accepted by every role that trusts the provider, so each role's trust policy would need per-person or per-group conditions.
- **The account number in the AWS client ID.** It names the account by a number nobody reads, and puts the number in every token and log line.
- **`sub` as the e-mail address or directory account.** Readable, but it changes on rename and puts personal data into every log line.
- **Upstream multi-factor authentication only, or checked once at the front.** Slow, and a compromised front could claim a fresh factor in every boundary.
- **A relying-party ID per host.** A person would register a passkey for every environment.
- **Serving the cached groups indefinitely during an outage.** No lock-out, but a removed person would keep access for as long as the directory is down.
- **Break-glass credentials as durable data.** A page could store them, but a damaged store would take the way in with it, and a restore would rewind them.
- **A recovery password, as v1's SRP-6a.** A reusable secret that must be stored, rotated and guarded, and the one password in a product that stores none.
- **Recovery codes.** Printed reusable secrets with the same problems, and no proof that a person holds them at the moment of use.
- **A ServiceAccount token checked by a cluster's API server.** Recovery would depend on the cluster, which may be the thing that failed, and would not exist on the other runtimes.
- **Hardware keys for everyone.** A key to buy and lose per person, and not every sign-in can use one; AAL2 is enough for ordinary access.
- **Synced passkeys at the privileged tier.** The account that syncs the passkey would be a path to the credential for the grants that matter most.
- **Attestation required for every staff passkey.** Synced passkeys give none, so phone and laptop passkeys would be refused.
- **Enterprise attestation.** It identifies one key by its serial number and ties a person's keys together across sites; a purchase register gives an inventory without it.
- **Class from BS alone.** BS changes over a credential's life and BE does not, so BS cannot decide what a credential is.
- **Trusting an upstream `hwk` for the privileged tier.** It is a statement of the upstream, absent unless requested, and sluis cannot check it.
- **Checking the signature counter of synced passkeys.** They report 0, so the check would refuse real sign-ins and prove nothing about a clone.
- **Switching the tiers on now.** The attestation verifier and the upstream `claims` request are not built, so a rule would lock out the people it protects; the class is recorded now and the rule is a policy commit later.
- **Enrolling a hardware key on an upstream sign-in alone.** A stolen synced-passkey session could then add the attacker's own allowlisted key.
- **Directory approval of the first hardware key.** The directory cannot say whose device a key is, and a directory change needs no hardware proof.
- **Trusting any pinned root for any AAGUID.** A chain valid to another model's root would pass as the allowlisted model.
- **Hardware keys without attestation.** A software authenticator could then be registered as a hardware key.
- **Only hardware keys.** Losing both, or the place where both are kept, would leave no way back in; the pinned synced passkey covers that.
- **A recovery session with the operator's full scope.** It would turn the emergency door into a standing back door.
